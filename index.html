Small Prey, Big Whales

Field Note simulation • Pacific krill, seawater chemistry and evidence

Your mission

Maya is preparing Harbor Point’s “What Feeds a Giant?” exhibit. Her draft says: “As the ocean becomes more acidic, all plankton lose their shells. Whales then run out of food.”

Your team will investigate early survival of Pacific krill, an important prey species, and decide which parts of Maya’s claim the evidence can address.

Teaching model: Harbor Point is fictional. Every numerical treatment and survival result in this sandbox is illustrative or simulated. These are not Monterey Bay measurements, published experimental results, or forecasts. The model does not predict whale populations.

Before you begin: Meet Pacific krill

Krill are small, shrimp-like marine crustaceans. Pacific krill (Euphausia pacifica) live in the North Pacific, including the California Current ecosystem. They are zooplankton: animals that can swim but whose distribution is strongly influenced by currents.

Krill transfer energy through marine food webs. Blue whales primarily eat krill. Humpback whales also eat krill, along with small schooling fish. This investigation focuses on the connection to blue whales, but the organisms in your tanks are early larvae—not a model of the dense patches of larger krill that feeding whales seek.

Why study larvae?

Krill hatch from eggs and pass through several larval stages before becoming juveniles and adults. Early life stages can respond differently from later stages. A published study of Pacific krill from Puget Sound found that egg hatching was relatively robust across tested pH conditions, while lower pH reduced larval development and survival. That research motivates our question; its results are not the invented dataset used here.

We begin after hatching, with 20 newly hatched larvae in each tank. We measure how many remain alive. We do not measure egg hatch, developmental stage, adult reproduction, or whale survival.

Follow the chemistry

When more carbon dioxide (CO₂) dissolves in seawater, chemical reactions increase hydrogen ions (H⁺), lowering pH. Ocean acidification means a shift toward lower pH; seawater can become more acidic while remaining basic. All three model treatments have pH above 7.

Changing seawater chemistry can challenge an animal’s internal chemical balance and physiological processes. This sandbox tests a survival response; it does not identify the specific physiological cause. Keep temperature, salinity, food, light and starting life stage constant so you can compare the CO₂ treatments.

How to read the tank illustrations

Each card represents one independent tank. Each small drawing represents one living larva in that tank. The stylized oval represents its body, and the projecting lines represent appendages. The drawing is enlarged, simplified, and not an anatomically complete image of a particular larval stage.

At day 0, every tank has 20 larval symbols. Later observations show the survivors; the accompanying count is the accessible numerical equivalent. Missing symbols indicate larvae that did not survive in this teaching model. Their absence does not depict predation or shell dissolution. The blue tank background does not encode acidity.

Count, then compare: There are three independent tanks per treatment and nine tanks overall. Animals sharing a tank are not independent treatment replicates. Calculate survival within each tank, then compare the three tank percentages within each treatment.

Your investigation question

How does increasing dissolved CO₂, with its associated changes in seawater chemistry, affect modeled Pacific krill larval survival over 14 days?

1. Predict and plan

Compare three model treatments: reference CO₂ (pH 8.1), elevated CO₂ (pH 7.9), and higher CO₂ (pH 7.7). These are illustrative conditions, not dated future scenarios. Each treatment has three independent tanks, each beginning with 20 newly hatched larvae.

In this simplified model, assigned pH stays constant within each treatment. Other conditions are held constant. Real coastal water chemistry varies, and pH alone does not describe every aspect of seawater chemistry.

2. Work with your observations

Tank survival (%) = living larvae ÷ 20 starting larvae × 100. For example, 17 survivors represent 85% survival. The treatment mean is the sum of its three tank survival percentages divided by three.

The fixed teaching dataset stays the same when you repeat observations. Day 7 and day 14 are separate assigned observations. Use the raw-data download for spreadsheet calculations; treatment means are not included in it.

Check your reasoning after writing

The invented dataset shows lower larval survival in the higher-CO₂ treatments. There is also variation among tanks, and some mortality occurs in the reference group. The results address early larval survival in this model, not all plankton, shell dissolution, development rate, or adult krill abundance. A potential connection to whale food is a hypothesis requiring more evidence.

To investigate that connection, you would need information about survival and reproduction across krill life stages, field abundance, food and other environmental conditions, and the location and density of krill patches available to blue whales. A tank result alone cannot establish that whales will run out of food.
